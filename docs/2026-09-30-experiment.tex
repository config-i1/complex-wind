\section{Purpose}

The paper models wind as a single complex variable, combining speed and direction, with complex
ARIMA (cARIMA). This note describes the first forecasting experiment on real data and its results.
The experiment asks whether cARIMA gives better point forecasts of 10-minute wind than two
simple alternatives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{persistence}, which forecasts that the wind stays as it was at the last observation;
  \item pairs of ordinary (real-valued) ARIMA models, one for each of two components of the wind,
    estimated independently of each other.
\end{itemize}
Forecasts are made from 10 minutes to 6 hours ahead. They are evaluated on speed, on direction
and on the wind vector as a whole.

The note first describes the data (Section~\ref{sec:data}) and how the wind is modelled
(Section~\ref{sec:models}), then the design of the forecasting study
(Section~\ref{sec:design}). The results are in two parts: the models estimated on the first
window of data (Section~\ref{sec:estimates}) and the forecast accuracy
(Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source.} The Kaggle ``Wind Turbine SCADA'' dataset, file \code{T1.csv}. SCADA
    (supervisory control and data acquisition) is the monitoring system of a wind turbine; it
    records measurements at the turbine, here every 10 minutes. The file covers one turbine in
    Turkey for the whole of 2018 and contains wind speed (m/s) and wind direction (degrees).
    More details are in \code{data/README.md}.
  \item \textbf{Missing values.} A complete 10-minute grid for 2018 has 52,560 time points; the
    file has 50,530 of them. The 2,030 missing points include three gaps longer than 3 days.
  \item \textbf{Preparation} (\code{R/02\_prepare\_data.R}):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item the data are put on the complete 10-minute grid, with the missing points left empty;
    \item gaps of at most 36 points (6 hours) are filled by linear interpolation of the two
      Cartesian coordinates of the wind vector, $\Re Z_t$ and $\Im Z_t$ (defined in
      Section~\ref{sec:complex});
    \item the 10 zero speeds are replaced by 0.1\,m/s, so that the logarithm of speed exists.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The 10-minute changes of wind are heavy-tailed: most changes are small, but some are very large.
Table~\ref{tab:kurt} gives the kurtosis $\E(x - \E x)^4/(\operatorname{Var} x)^2$ of the changes
between consecutive observations. It equals 3 for a normal distribution; larger values mean
heavier tails. All four changes are far from normal. About 3\% of the direction changes exceed
$30^\circ$, and 0.5\% exceed $90^\circ$. The changes of the Cartesian coordinates combine changes of speed and of direction, and
$\Re Z_t$ has the heaviest tails of all: a sudden turn at high speed moves the wind vector a long way.

Figure~\ref{fig:hist} shows the same changes as histograms with the counts on a log scale, so that
the tails are visible; a normal distribution would appear as a parabola.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Left, change of log speed.} The distribution is sharply peaked at zero: half of the
    changes are within $\pm 0.06$ (about $\pm 6\%$ of the speed), with a standard deviation of
    0.16. But the counts decay roughly linearly on the log scale, i.e.\ exponentially rather than
    like a normal distribution, and reach changes of $-1.6$ and $+2.4$ (speeds falling to a fifth or
    rising elevenfold within 10 minutes). Such large relative changes happen at low speeds: in all 66
changes larger than 1 in absolute value, one of the two speeds is below 3.7\,m/s.
  \item \textbf{Middle, change of the recorded angle.} Besides the central peak there are two side
    peaks near $\pm 2\pi$. They are not real turns of the wind but the artificial jumps that occur
    when the direction crosses $\pm\pi$ (696 times; Section~\ref{sec:diff}). Because of them the
    standard deviation is 0.71\,rad.
  \item \textbf{Right, shortest-arc change of direction.} With the jumps removed, the side peaks
    disappear and the standard deviation falls to 0.25\,rad ($14^\circ$). The centre is again very
    narrow (half of the changes are within about $\pm 3^\circ$), and the tails are heavy and extend
    to $\pm\pi$: there are 63 turns of more than $150^\circ$ within 10 minutes.
\end{itemize}
These heavy tails motivate the heavy-tailed error distribution in
Section~\ref{sec:cgnorm}, and the side peaks motivate the unwrapping of the angle in
Section~\ref{sec:diff}.

\section{How the wind is modelled}\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Wind as a complex number}\label{sec:complex}

With speed $v_t > 0$ and direction angle $\theta_t \in (-\pi, \pi]$ (in radians), the wind at time
$t$ is
\begin{equation}
  Z_t = v_t e^{\iu\theta_t} = v_t\cos\theta_t + \iu\, v_t\sin\theta_t .
\end{equation}
The modulus $|Z_t|$ is the speed and the argument $\Arg Z_t$ is the direction. $\Re Z_t$ and
$\Im Z_t$ are the Cartesian coordinates of the wind vector. They are not the zonal and meridional
components used in meteorology, which are measured along the east and north axes; here the angle
is simply the recorded direction.

\subsection{Complex ARIMA}

The cARIMA$(p,d,q)$ model for a complex series $y_t$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:carima}
  \Bigl(1 - \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j B^j\Bigr)(1-B)^d\, y_t
  = \beta_0 + \Bigl(1 + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j B^j\Bigr)\varepsilon_t,
  \qquad \phi_j, \theta_j, \beta_0 \in \mathbb{C},
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the backshift operator ($B y_t = y_{t-1}$), $p$ and $q$ are the autoregressive (AR) and
moving average (MA) orders, and $d$ is the order of differencing. With $d = 1$ the constant
$\beta_0$ is a \emph{drift}: a fixed complex change added at every step. With $d = 0$ it
determines the mean of the series, $\beta_0 / (1 - \sum_j \phi_j)$.

Multiplying by a complex coefficient $\phi = a + \iu b = r e^{\iu\alpha}$ \emph{scales} by
$r = |\phi|$ and \emph{rotates} by $\alpha = \Arg\phi$. For the pair $(\Re y, \Im y)$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\phi y) \\ \Im(\phi y) \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix} \Re y \\ \Im y \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
so cARIMA is a bivariate VARIMA whose coefficient matrices are restricted to this form: 2 real
parameters per lag instead of 4.

The models are estimated by maximum likelihood with \code{complex::clm()}. It fits
\eqref{eq:carima} for the series in levels: the observations lost to differencing and lags at the
start of the sample are filled in by extrapolation, and the forecasts are produced directly for
$y_t$ rather than for its differences.

\subsection{The error distribution and non-circularity}\label{sec:error}

In the basic model, the error $\varepsilon_t$ is \emph{complex normal}. For a complex random
variable with zero mean, two second moments are needed, and we use the terms of
\citet{svetunkov2024}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{conjugate variance} $\sigma^2 = \E(\varepsilon_t\bar\varepsilon_t) =
    \E|\varepsilon_t|^2$, computed with the complex conjugate; it is real and measures the overall
    size of the error;
  \item the \emph{direct variance} $\varsigma^2 = \E(\varepsilon_t^2)$, computed without the
    conjugate (elsewhere called the pseudo-variance); it is complex in general and describes the
    shape of the error cloud in the plane.
\end{itemize}
Together they give the covariance matrix of the real and imaginary parts:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(\Re\varepsilon_t) = \tfrac12(\sigma^2 + \Re\varsigma^2), \quad
  \operatorname{Var}(\Im\varepsilon_t) = \tfrac12(\sigma^2 - \Re\varsigma^2), \quad
  \operatorname{Cov}(\Re\varepsilon_t, \Im\varepsilon_t) = \tfrac12\Im\varsigma^2 .
\end{equation}
With $\varsigma^2 = 0$ the error is \emph{circular}: the two parts have the same variance and are
uncorrelated, so the cloud of errors is a circle. With $\varsigma^2 \neq 0$ it is
\emph{non-circular}: the cloud is an ellipse. For wind, non-circularity means that the changes of
the wind vector are larger along some directions than others. We measure it by the ratio of the
two variances,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noncirc}
  \kappa = \frac{|\varsigma^2|}{\sigma^2} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
which is 0 for a circular error and 1 for an error that always lies on one line. A pair of
independent real models, one for $\Re Z_t$ and one for $\Im Z_t$, ignores the correlation between
the two errors.

\subsection{The complex generalised normal distribution}\label{sec:cgnorm}

Because the changes of wind are heavy-tailed (Table~\ref{tab:kurt}), the models are also
estimated with a \emph{complex generalised normal} error (\citealp{novey2010};
\code{distribution = "dcgnorm"} in \code{clm()}). For the error $e = z - \mu$, with conjugate
scale $\alpha^2 > 0$, direct scale $\psi^2 \in \mathbb{C}$ ($|\psi^2| < \alpha^2$) and shape
$\beta > 0$, the density is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cgnorm}
  f(z) = \frac{2\beta}{\pi\,\Gamma(1/\beta)\, 2^{1/\beta}\sqrt{\alpha^4 - |\psi^2|^2}}
         \exp\Bigl(-\tfrac12 Q^\beta\Bigr),
  \qquad
  Q = \frac{2\bigl(\alpha^2|e|^2 - \Re(\overline{\psi^2}\, e^2)\bigr)}{\alpha^4 - |\psi^2|^2}.
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta = 1$ gives the complex normal distribution with conjugate variance $\alpha^2$ and
    direct variance $\psi^2$. A shape $\beta < 1$ gives heavier tails ($\beta = 0.5$ is similar to
    the Laplace distribution); $\beta > 1$ gives lighter tails.
  \item For other $\beta$, the conjugate and direct variances equal $\alpha^2$ and $\psi^2$
    multiplied by $2^{1/\beta}\Gamma(2/\beta) / (2\Gamma(1/\beta))$. In the package the two scales
    are called \code{scale} and \code{pseudoscale}.
  \item The shape $\beta$ is estimated together with the ARIMA coefficients. For any given values
    of these, the maximum likelihood estimates of the two scales have a closed form, so
    they are substituted into the likelihood (``concentrated out'') and not searched for
    numerically.
\end{itemize}
With heavy tails, a few very large changes can pull the estimates of a normal model, in
particular the drift. A heavy-tailed likelihood gives these observations less weight.

\subsection{Why logarithms}\label{sec:log}

Besides $Z_t$ itself, the models are applied to the complex logarithm of the wind,
\begin{equation}
  \log Z_t = \log v_t + \iu\,\tilde\theta_t ,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde\theta_t$ is the unwrapped direction angle (Section~\ref{sec:diff}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item The logarithm \emph{separates speed and direction}: the real part is log speed and the
    imaginary part is the angle. A cARIMA for $\log Z_t$ is still one complex model: the two parts
    are linked through the complex coefficients and the error covariance.
  \item Changes of speed are roughly \emph{proportional} to the speed (larger when it is windy), so
    log speed is a more natural scale than speed.
  \item Forecasts are transformed back by $\hat Z = \exp(\widehat{\log Z})$, which always gives a
    positive speed and a valid direction.
  \item $\exp(\widehat{\log Z})$ is not the expected value of $Z$; no correction for this is made.
  \item The drift of a model for $\log Z_t$ with $d = 1$ has a direct meaning: $\Re\beta_0$ is a
    steady percentage change of speed, and $\Im\beta_0$ is a steady rotation of the direction, in
    radians per 10 minutes. There is no physical reason for the wind to keep turning in one
    direction for weeks, so models for $\log Z_t$ are also fitted without the constant.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Differences and unwrapping of the angle}\label{sec:diff}

\begin{itemize}
  \item At 10-minute resolution the wind is very persistent: over several weeks its level wanders
    without returning to a stable mean. The models are therefore fitted mainly to changes,
    $d = 1$. In logarithms the differences have a clear meaning:
    \begin{equation}
      (1-B)\log Z_t = \log\frac{v_t}{v_{t-1}} + \iu\,(\tilde\theta_t - \tilde\theta_{t-1}),
    \end{equation}
    the log ratio of consecutive speeds plus $\iu$ times the change of direction.
  \item The recorded angle jumps by about $2\pi$ whenever the direction crosses $\pm\pi$. For
    example, a turn from $179^\circ$ to $-179^\circ$ is a change of $2^\circ$, but the recorded
    values differ by $358^\circ$. This happens 696 times in \code{T1.csv}. In differences these
    jumps would appear as huge artificial shocks (Figure~\ref{fig:hist}, middle).
  \item The angle is therefore \emph{unwrapped} within each estimation window $t = 1, \dots, T$:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:unwrap}
      \tilde\theta_1 = \theta_1, \qquad
      \tilde\theta_t = \tilde\theta_{t-1} + \wrap(\theta_t - \theta_{t-1}), \qquad
      \wrap(x) = \operatorname{atan2}(\sin x, \cos x) \in (-\pi, \pi].
    \end{equation}
    $\wrap(\cdot)$ returns the equivalent angle in $(-\pi, \pi]$, so each step of $\tilde\theta_t$
    is the \emph{shortest-arc} change of direction, and $\tilde\theta_t$ can leave $(-\pi, \pi]$.
    Forecasts of the direction are wrapped back into $(-\pi, \pi]$ for evaluation.
  \item The unwrapped angle is used in $\log Z_t$ and in the real benchmark models for the
    angle (Section~\ref{sec:selection}). $Z_t$ itself needs no unwrapping: it is continuous when
    the direction crosses $\pm\pi$, because $\cos$ and $\sin$ are periodic.
  \item For $Z_t$, $d = 0$ is also a plausible choice: the wind vector could revert to a mean.
    One set of models chooses $d \in \{0, 1\}$ automatically (Section~\ref{sec:selection}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Design of the forecasting study}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Rolling origin}

The forecasts are evaluated with a \emph{rolling origin}: the models are repeatedly estimated on a
window of past data and forecast the next 6 hours, for many different ends of the window.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{forecast origin} is the last observation used for estimation. The estimation
    window is the 4 weeks up to the origin ($T = 4032$ observations), and the forecast
    \emph{horizon} is $h = 1, \dots, 36$ steps ahead (10 minutes to 6 hours).
  \item An origin is used only if its window has no missing values and its 6-hour horizon has no
    missing values and no values from interpolations longer than 3 points (30 minutes). So the
    forecasts are compared with values that were actually observed, or nearly so.
  \item The first window that satisfies this (2018-01-30 14:40 to 2018-02-27 14:30) is the
    \emph{selection window}. It is used only to choose the model orders
    (Section~\ref{sec:selection}).
  \item 300 forecast origins are spread evenly over all later usable origins, from 2018-02-27 14:40
    to 2018-12-31 17:50. They are spread evenly over the times of day, but not over the months:
    months with more gaps have fewer usable origins. There are about 40 origins in each month from
    March to September (except June, with 5), none in October, 14 in November and 27 in December
    (plus 2 at the end of February).
  \item At every origin, the parameters of all models are estimated again on that origin's
    window; only the orders stay fixed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Choice of model orders}\label{sec:selection}

The orders are chosen once, on the selection window, and kept for all 300 origins.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{cARIMA with $d = 1$ (grid search).} All models with $p, q \in \{0, \dots, 3\}$ are
    estimated, and the one with the lowest AICc is chosen. AICc is Akaike's information criterion
    with a correction for small samples: it balances the fit (log-likelihood) against the number
    of parameters.
  \item \textbf{cARIMA with automatic choice of $d$ and of the constant} (\code{auto.carima()} in
    the \code{complex} package):
    \[
      \text{\code{auto.carima(y, orders = list(ar = 3, i = 1, ma = 3), constant = NULL)}}.
    \]
    For each $d \in \{0, 1\}$ it runs a \emph{stepwise} search: starting from cARIMA$(0,d,0)$, it
    fits all models whose $p$ and $q$ differ by at most one from the current model, moves to the
    best of them, and stops when AICc no longer improves ($p, q \le 3$). The constant is first
    included for $d = 0$ and excluded for $d = 1$; the best model for each $d$ is then also tried
    with the opposite choice, and the search continues from there. Models with different $d$ lose
    different numbers of initial observations, so AICc is computed on a common sample that drops
    the first few observations for all of them.
  \item \textbf{Starting values.} From its default starting values, \code{clm()} sometimes stops
    at a poor local maximum of the likelihood; for example, a larger model ending with a lower
    likelihood than a smaller model nested in it. Therefore every cARIMA model is estimated from
    several starting values, and the fit with the highest likelihood is kept:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item in the grid search, the default and the estimates of the nested models
        $(p-1, q)$ and $(p, q-1)$, with zeros for the new coefficient;
      \item in the rolling origin, the default and the estimates from the selection window.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Real benchmarks.} \code{forecast::auto.arima()} with $d = 1$ (a stepwise search of
    the same kind for a real series, by AICc), and \code{smooth::auto.msarima()}. The latter also
    chooses $d$ itself, so it is applied to the differenced series, and the chosen model is then
    fitted to the series with $d = 1$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Error measures}

For origin $t$ and horizon $h$, with forecast $\hat Z_{t+h|t}$ and actual value $Z_{t+h}$, three
errors are computed in the original units:
\begin{align}
  e^{\text{speed}}_{t,h} &= |\hat Z_{t+h|t}| - |Z_{t+h}| \quad \text{(m/s)}, \\
  e^{\text{angle}}_{t,h} &= \tfrac{180}{\pi}\wrap\bigl(\Arg\hat Z_{t+h|t} - \Arg Z_{t+h}\bigr)
    \quad \text{(degrees)}, \\
  e^{\text{vector}}_{t,h} &= |\hat Z_{t+h|t} - Z_{t+h}| \quad \text{(m/s)}.
\end{align}
The angle error is the shortest-arc difference, between $-180^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. The vector
error is the length of the difference between the forecast and the actual wind vectors, so it
combines errors of speed and direction. Each measure is summarised by the root mean squared error
over the 300 origins, $\RMSE_h = \bigl(\frac{1}{300}\sum_t e_{t,h}^2\bigr)^{1/2}$, for each
horizon, and over all 36 horizons together (column ``$h = 1$--$36$'').

origin the parameters are estimated again at every origin, so these values illustrate the models
rather than being used for the forecasts. Standard errors are not reported: \code{summary()} fails

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Orders.} All models are small: at most one AR and three MA terms. With $d$ chosen
    automatically, the models for $Z_t$ have $d = 0$ and those for $\log Z_t$ have $d = 1$.
    \code{auto\_carima\_logZ\_cgn} is the same specification as \code{carima\_logZ\_cgn\_nd}:
    $(0,1,2)$ without a constant.
  \item \textbf{Heavy tails.} The estimated shape of the generalised normal is
    $\hat\beta \approx 0.21$ for $\log Z_t$ and $\hat\beta \approx 0.41$ for $Z_t$, far below the
    normal value of 1. The generalised normal raises the log-likelihood by about 5,000 for
    $\log Z_t$ and 1,500 for $Z_t$.
  \item \textbf{Drift of the direction.} For $\log Z_t$ with a normal error, the imaginary part of
    the constant is $0.0122$\,rad ($0.70^\circ$) per 10 minutes in \code{carima\_logZ}, a rotation
    of about $25^\circ$ over the 6-hour horizon. The real model for the angle (\code{arima\_angle},
    Table~\ref{tab:bench}) has the same drift, $0.0121$. With the generalised normal the drift is
    ten times smaller, $0.0012$. \code{auto.carima()} keeps the constant for $\log Z_t$ with the
    normal error and drops it with the generalised normal.
  \item \textbf{$Z_t$ with $d = 0$.} $\hat\phi_1 \approx 0.993$--$0.994$, with an argument close
    to zero: the model is close to a random walk and does not rotate. A deviation from the mean
    halves in $\log 0.5 / \log|\hat\phi_1| \approx 100$--$120$ steps, i.e.\ 16--20 hours (the
    \emph{half-life}). The implied mean wind vector $\beta_0/(1 - \phi_1)$ is $-2.63 + 1.14\iu$
    (normal) and $-2.16 + 3.49\iu$ (generalised normal), 2.9 and 4.1\,m/s long. The mean
    \emph{speed} in the selection window is 8.5\,m/s, but the mean \emph{vector} is only 3.5\,m/s
    long, because winds from different directions cancel. So at long horizons these forecasts
    move towards a short vector, i.e.\ a low speed.
  \item \textbf{A sub-optimal fit in the order choice.} Refitting \code{carima\_logZ\_nd},
    $(1,1,2)$, gives a log-likelihood of $492.51$, higher than the $489.91$ found in the grid
    search. The best fit has $\hat\theta_2 = 0$ exactly, which suggests that the optimiser stayed
    at the starting values taken from the nested $(1,1,1)$ model. The grid search for this model
    thus compared a sub-optimal fit, and $(1,1,1)$ may have been the better choice. All other
    models reproduce the grid-search log-likelihood to within 0.02.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:resid} describes the residuals of the same models on the selection window: the
standard deviations of their real and imaginary parts, the correlation between the two, and the
non-circularity \eqref{eq:noncirc}. $\kappa$ is estimated by the ratio of the sample direct and
conjugate variances of the centred residuals
$\tilde\varepsilon_t = \hat\varepsilon_t - \frac1T\sum_s\hat\varepsilon_s$:
$\hat\kappa = \bigl|\sum_t\tilde\varepsilon_t^2\bigr| / \sum_t|\tilde\varepsilon_t|^2$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The values are almost the same for all models of the same series: the residuals depend on
    the series much more than on the orders or the distribution.
  \item $\hat\kappa$ is 0.27--0.33 for all models, so the errors are clearly non-circular: the
    direct variance is about a third of the conjugate variance.
  \item For $Z_t$, the two coordinates have different spreads (0.90 and 0.80\,m/s) and are
    positively correlated (0.31), so the error ellipse is tilted: the wind vector changes most
    along a diagonal of the plane, i.e.\ along a prevailing direction.
  \item For $\log Z_t$, the imaginary part (direction) has a larger standard deviation, 0.26\,rad
    or about $15^\circ$, than the real part (log speed), 0.20, and the two are almost
    uncorrelated. Here the non-circularity comes mainly from the different spreads of speed and
    direction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Findings}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No model beats persistence on all three measures.} The gains over persistence are
    small, at most about 3\% over all horizons (vector: 3.455 vs 3.567), as is typical for
    10-minute wind forecast from its own past only. Poorly specified models are up to about 10\%
    worse than persistence.
  \item \textbf{Short horizons ($h = 1$--$3$):} cARIMA for $\log Z$ with the generalised normal
    error (\code{carima\_logZ\_cgn}, \code{carima\_logZ\_cgn\_nd}) is best for the vector error and
    for speed.
  \item \textbf{The drift rotates the forecasts.} With a normal error, the constant of a model for
    $\log Z$ rotates the forecast direction at a steady rate. \code{carima\_logZ} and
    \code{auto\_carima\_logZ} are among the worst models for direction (about $40^\circ$ against
    $38.6^\circ$ for persistence over all horizons) and for the vector error. Without the constant,
    \code{carima\_logZ\_nd} has the best direction forecasts of all models ($38.21^\circ$). The
    generalised normal largely removes the problem even with a constant, because its drift
    estimate is much smaller.
  \item \textbf{$Z$ with $d = 0$:} \code{auto\_carima\_z\_cgn} has the lowest vector error of all
    models over all horizons (3.455, against 3.567 for persistence and 3.522 for the best pair of
    real models, \code{arima\_re\_im}). Its advantage comes from $h = 12$ onwards. It is worse for
    speed (2.352 against 2.282), because its forecasts move towards the short mean wind vector.
    \code{auto\_carima\_z}, with the normal error, behaves the same way and has the worst speed
    forecasts (2.466).
  \item \textbf{Speed alone} is forecast best by \code{arima\_speed\_angle} from $h = 6$ onwards
    (2.214 over all horizons), the real model fitted to speed directly.
  \item \textbf{Heavy tails matter:} $\hat\beta \approx 0.2$ for $\log Z$ and $0.4$ for $Z$, and
    the generalised normal helps most where the drift of the normal model distorts the forecasts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and next steps}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Only point forecasts are evaluated: no predictive distributions, no tests of the
    differences in accuracy (e.g.\ Diebold--Mariano), and no other error measures (e.g.\ MAE).
  \item One turbine and one year. The orders are chosen on a single window in February and kept
    for the whole year.
  \item The forecasts for $\log Z$ are transformed back without a correction for the bias of
    $\exp(\cdot)$.
  \item The order of \code{carima\_logZ\_nd} was chosen from a sub-optimal fit
    (Section~\ref{sec:estimates}).
  \item Not yet included: daily cycles (e.g.\ Fourier terms), VAR and VARMA benchmarks, which are
    needed to test the argument that cARIMA is more parsimonious, forecasts of turbine power, and
    other datasets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducing the results}\label{sec:reproduce}

\begin{verbatim}
Rscript R/02_prepare_data.R              # needs data/raw/T1.csv
Rscript R/03_select_orders.R [model ...] # no arguments = all models
Rscript R/04_rolling_origin.R [model ...]
Rscript R/05_evaluate.R
Rscript R/06_parameters.R
\end{verbatim}

\noindent \code{data/README.md} explains how to download \code{T1.csv}. Shared code is in
\code{R/functions.R}. \code{03\_select\_orders.R} chooses the orders on the selection window,
\code{04\_rolling\_origin.R} produces the forecasts, \code{05\_evaluate.R} computes the RMSE tables,
and \code{06\_parameters.R} produces Tables~\ref{tab:params}--\ref{tab:bench}. Scripts \code{03} and
\code{04} can be run for selected models only; their results are merged with the existing ones. On
4 cores, the order choice and the 300 forecasts of the four \code{auto\_carima} models took 23
minutes and 2.8 hours.
